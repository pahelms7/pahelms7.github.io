What Is a Sports Chiropractor? How It Differs from General Chiropractic

By Dr. Paul Helms | 7 min read | Updated August 2026

A sports chiropractor is a chiropractor with additional training in movement mechanics, soft tissue treatment, and the injury patterns that show up in bodies under load. A sports chiropractor still adjusts the spine and joints, and also treats the muscles, tendons, and nerves around them, then works on the movement pattern that created the problem.

The name puts a lot of people off, and it should not. Whether you are a masters swimmer with a cranky shoulder or someone who simply wants to lift a laundry basket without your lower back objecting, what you need is the same. Someone who checks how the whole thing moves before deciding what to treat. Here is what that actually looks like, in plain English.

What Is a Sports Chiropractor?

Start with what is shared. Every chiropractor, sports-focused or not, holds a Doctor of Chiropractic degree and is licensed to assess and treat the spine, joints, and the nervous system. Adjustments are common ground.

What a sports chiropractor adds is a second half of the toolkit and a different set of questions. The tools include soft tissue techniques such as Active Release Technique, which is a massage and movement-based method for treating problems with muscles, tendons, ligaments, fascia, and nerves, and often dry needling, which uses a thin filament needle to release a tight band inside a muscle. The questions are about load: what were you asking that joint to do, how often, and with what around it helping or not helping.

In my practice at Helms Performance, the shoulder is the clearest example. Shoulder pain that has not responded to anything is very often a neck and mid-back problem wearing a shoulder costume. Adjusting the shoulder alone gives a good afternoon. Treating the neck, the mid-back, and the soft tissue that has tightened around all of it is what gives a good month.

In one sentence

Same license, wider toolkit. A sports chiropractor treats the joint, the tissue around it, and the movement that overloaded both.

Do You Have to Be an Athlete to See a Sports Chiropractor?

No, and most of the people in our schedule are not. The sports lens is really a movement lens, and everybody moves.

  • Desk workers with neck stiffness, tension headaches, and a mid-back that stopped rotating years ago.
  • Runners and cyclists with hip and knee issues that flare every training block.
  • Adults in their 60s and 70s whose stiffness has quietly narrowed what they are willing to do.
  • Parents carrying, lifting, and twisting all day on one side.
  • People who have tried rest and stretching and watched the same problem come back anyway.

Dr. Helms has worked with patients ranging from weekend walkers to professional athletes in the NFL and NBA. He brings the same attention to a 70-year-old managing sciatica as to a competitive athlete before a season, and the practice is built to welcome both.

What Does a Sports Chiropractor Actually Do in a Visit?

A visit at Helms Performance is one hour, one-on-one with Dr. Helms. Treatment starts on day one in most cases, so you are not paying for an appointment that only produces a follow-up booking.

  • Step 1. History. What is happening, how long, what makes it better or worse, and what you have already tried.
  • Step 2. Movement assessment. How the whole chain moves, not only the area you pointed at.
  • Step 3. Hands-on evaluation. Joint mobility, tissue tenderness, and nerve function in the region involved.
  • Step 4. Treatment. Adjustments where a joint is restricted, soft tissue work such as ART or myofascial release where the tissue is, and dry needling when a trigger point is driving it.
  • Step 5. The plan. What is going on, what we are doing about it, and roughly how many visits it usually takes.

What Conditions Does a Sports Chiropractor Treat?

  • Lower back pain, including sciatica that radiates into the leg.
  • Neck pain, stiffness, and headaches that originate in the neck.
  • Shoulder pain, rotator cuff irritation, and overhead restriction.
  • Hip pain and hip flexor tightness from sitting or from running.
  • Knee pain, assessed from the hip and ankle as well as the knee itself.
  • Sprains, strains, and overuse injuries.
  • Joint stiffness that limits ordinary things such as reaching a top shelf.

How Do You Choose a Sports Chiropractor?

Four questions worth asking before you book anywhere:

  • Who treats me? Some offices assess with the doctor and hand you to an assistant. Ask.
  • How long is the visit? A movement assessment plus treatment does not fit into ten minutes.
  • What soft tissue training do you have? Certification in Active Release Technique and a dry needling license are specific, checkable answers.
  • How many visits, roughly? A good answer is a range with a reason behind it, not an open-ended package.

One caution. If your pain came on suddenly, followed a fall or a crash, or comes with numbness, weakness, or fever, get evaluated before booking any hands-on care. If Dr. Helms finds something that belongs with another specialist, he will say so and help coordinate the referral.

Common Questions

Sports Chiropractor FAQs

What does a sports chiropractor do? +
A sports chiropractor assesses how you move, treats restricted joints with adjustments, treats the surrounding muscles, tendons, and nerves with soft tissue techniques, and then addresses the movement pattern that caused the problem. The goal is not only to make today's pain quieter but to change the load that produced it.
How is a sports chiropractor different from a regular chiropractor? +
Both hold the same Doctor of Chiropractic license and both adjust joints. A sports chiropractor adds training in soft tissue techniques such as Active Release Technique and dry needling, a deeper focus on movement mechanics, and experience with the injury patterns that show up in bodies under load. The extra tools help whether or not you play a sport.
Do I have to be an athlete to see a sports chiropractor? +
No. Most patients at Helms Performance are not competitive athletes. They are desk workers with neck tension, parents carrying a lot right now, runners in the middle of a training block, and adults in their 60s and 70s who want stairs and gardening to stop hurting. A movement-informed approach helps any body that has to do something.
How many visits will I need? +
It depends on the problem and how long it has been going on. Acute issues with a clear cause often settle within three to five visits. Long-standing problems with several contributing factors take longer. Dr. Helms gives you a realistic estimate at the first visit so you know what you are agreeing to before you commit to a plan.
