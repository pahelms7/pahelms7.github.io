TMJ Exercises for Jaw Pain: A Simple Daily Routine

By Dr. Paul Helms | 7 min read | Updated August 2026

TMJ exercises retrain the jaw to open and close on a straight, even path instead of a painful or clicking one. A 5 minute routine of controlled opening, gentle resistance, and tongue position, done twice a day, eases jaw pain for many people over 3 to 4 weeks.

Jaw pain has a way of following you through the day. Whether you are an athlete who clenches through a heavy lift or someone who grinds through a stressful week of meetings, it shows up in eating, talking, and sleeping. Small, frequent, low-effort work tends to help more than anything forceful.

In my practice at Helms Performance in Bethesda, MD, the detail patients are most surprised by is the neck. People arrive expecting me to work only on the jaw, and a good share of the improvement comes from the upper neck and head position instead. The two are mechanically linked.

In one sentence

These exercises train control, not force. Small movements done often beat big movements done hard.

What Do TMJ Exercises Actually Do?

The temporomandibular joint, or TMJ, is the hinge just in front of each ear where your jaw meets your skull. Inside it sits a small disc that should glide smoothly as you open. When the muscles around the joint pull unevenly, that glide gets rough, which is where clicking, catching, and pain tend to come from.

These exercises do three things. They train the jaw to open on a straight line rather than veering to one side, they build gentle control in the muscles that steady the joint, and they break the clenching habit that keeps the whole area loaded. Notice that none of those involve stretching the jaw open hard. Forcing range is the fastest way to irritate this joint.

Which TMJ Exercises Should You Start With?

Do these in front of a mirror to start, because seeing the jaw drift is what teaches you to correct it. Everything here should be comfortable. Pain means back off.

  1. Resting position. Lips together, teeth slightly apart, tongue resting on the roof of your mouth just behind the front teeth. This is where your jaw should sit all day. Check in on it a few times an hour.
  2. Controlled opening. Hold that tongue position and open slowly, only as far as the tongue stays on the roof of your mouth. Watch the mirror and keep the jaw straight. 6 slow repetitions.
  3. Gentle resisted opening. Place your thumb under your chin and open against light pressure, about the effort it takes to hold a coffee cup. Hold 5 seconds, 5 repetitions.
  4. Gentle resisted closing. Pinch your chin between thumb and finger and close against light pressure. Hold 5 seconds, 5 repetitions.
  5. Side-to-side control. Place an object about a quarter inch thick between your front teeth and move the jaw slowly left and right. 10 repetitions.
  6. Chin tuck. Sitting tall, draw your chin straight back to make a double chin without tipping your head. Hold 5 seconds, 10 repetitions. This is the neck piece, and it matters more than people expect.

The whole routine takes about 5 minutes. Twice a day is plenty. If your jaw feels tired afterward, that is fine. If it feels sore, use less pressure next time.

Why Does Your Neck Posture Change Your Jaw?

When your head drifts forward over a laptop, the muscles under your jaw are put on a stretch and the resting position of the lower jaw shifts backward. Your bite changes slightly, the joint sits differently, and the muscles that close the jaw end up working from a poor starting position all day.

This is why the chin tuck is on the list, and why treatment often includes the upper neck. If you also get headaches or stiffness at the base of the skull, our neck pain page is worth a look, because the two problems usually travel together.

What Should You Avoid While Your Jaw Settles?

The exercises help most when you stop feeding the problem the rest of the day.

  • Chewing gum. Hours of low-level loading on a joint you are trying to calm.
  • Hard and chewy foods. Bagels, tough steak, crusty bread, and ice. Cut food smaller for a few weeks.
  • Wide unsupported yawns. Rest a fist gently under the chin when you feel one coming.
  • Resting your chin in your hand. Common at a desk, and it pushes the joint sideways for long stretches.
  • Sleeping face down. This turns the head and loads one joint all night.
  • Nail biting and pen chewing. Small habits, steady load.

How Long Before TMJ Exercises Help?

Progress here is gradual, and the first thing to change is usually how the jaw moves rather than how much it hurts.

When Should You Stop And Get It Looked At?

Most jaw pain responds well to gentle work. A few situations need a professional rather than another week of exercises.

  • A jaw that locks, either stuck open or unable to open more than a couple of finger widths.
  • Pain that is clearly worse after two to three weeks of careful, gentle work.
  • Swelling, warmth, or a fever around the joint.
  • Worsening headaches, dizziness, or new ear symptoms such as ringing or fullness.
  • Jaw pain that started after a blow to the face or jaw.

Sorting out a jaw problem often takes more than one professional. A dentist looks at your bite, grinding, and whether a night guard would help, while we look at the joint, the muscles, and the neck. If you grind at night, please start with your dentist. Please do not try to diagnose the cause yourself, since jaw pain, ear pain, and headaches overlap a great deal.

Dr. Paul's Final Thoughts

Start with resting position. Lips together, teeth apart, tongue on the roof of your mouth. If you did nothing else on this list, that habit alone would help, because most jaws are quietly loaded all day.

Then add the 5 minute routine twice daily, keep every effort light, and drop the gum. Give it three or four weeks. If the jaw is no better, or if it locks at any point, come get it looked at rather than pushing through. Common Questions

TMJ Exercises FAQs

What exercises get rid of TMJ pain? +
The core set is controlled opening with the tongue on the roof of your mouth, gentle resisted opening and closing held 5 seconds, side-to-side control, and chin tucks for the neck. About 5 minutes, twice a day. All of them train control rather than stretching the jaw open, because forcing range tends to irritate this joint rather than help it.
How long does it take for TMJ exercises to work? +
Many people notice the jaw opening straighter within two weeks and less pain with eating and talking by weeks 3 to 4. Clicking usually improves more slowly than pain, and some clicking can remain even after the jaw feels comfortable. If four weeks of consistent gentle work changes nothing, it is worth getting evaluated.
Can TMJ exercises make jaw pain worse? +
They can if you use too much force or push into pain. The resistance in these exercises should be light, roughly the effort of holding a coffee cup, and nothing should be painful while you do it. Feeling tired afterward is fine. Feeling sore means use less pressure next time, or fewer repetitions.
Should I see a dentist or a chiropractor for TMJ? +
Often both, for different parts of the problem. A dentist evaluates your bite, night grinding, and whether a guard would help. We look at how the joint moves, the chewing muscles, and the upper neck, which frequently drives jaw symptoms. If you know you grind at night, start with your dentist.
Does TMJ pain go away on its own? +
Frequently it does, especially when it follows a stressful stretch or a period of heavy clenching. What tends to keep it going is the daily load: gum, chewy food, chin resting on a hand, and a head that sits forward at a desk. Changing those habits alongside the exercises is what usually settles it for good.
